Section 1

Why the system is built in phases

Eight companies move onto this system. Building everything at once and switching it all on in one day is how ERP projects fail.

You see it early

Each phase ends with something you can open and use. Changing a screen you have seen costs an afternoon. Changing one already carrying a year of data costs a great deal more.

The foundation is built once

Login, companies, users, permissions and the safety layer underneath are built before any business screen. Every module afterwards inherits them rather than reinventing them.

Your companies move one at a time

As you proposed. One company goes live, is reconciled, and only then does the next begin. A problem affects one business, not eight.

Phase 1 is the working core of the business

By the end of it you can sell, buy, take and make payments, see your chart of accounts and a statement for any account, and control stock — with users, roles and permissions around all of it. That is a system a company can run on.

Section 2

The foundation, before any module

None of this is visible as a feature, and all of it decides whether the system is still safe to change in three years.

Getting in — securely

Login and authentication · registration and onboarding for a new company · password security · protection against repeated guessing · optional two-step login for finance and administrator accounts.

Setting up your organisation

Company setup · branches · currencies and rates · number series for your documents · the settings that make the system yours rather than generic.

Administration

Users, roles and permissions · who may see cost prices, who may give a discount, who may post · company settings · the sign-in window per user you asked for.

The part you never see

Separation between companies · a full audit trail of who did what and when · error handling · automated testing · secure configuration. This is the layer that makes everything above it trustworthy.

Each business gets its own separate database

Not a shared one with a label on each row. When you resell this system, two customers who compete with each other are not sitting in the same tables — their data is in different databases entirely. A query for one business physically cannot reach another's.

It also means a single company can be backed up, restored or moved on its own, which is what makes migrating your eight companies one at a time practical.

Section 3

Four modules, each standing on its own

These are four separate parts of the system with their own screens, their own rules and their own boundaries. They talk to each other — none of them is inside another.

Module 1

Sales

Everything that goes out to a customer and the money that comes back.

  • Sales Invoice
  • Sales Return
  • Receipt Voucher
Owns: customers' documents, prices, what was sold
Module 2

Procurement

Everything that comes in from a supplier and the money that goes out.

  • Purchase Invoice
  • Purchase Return
  • Payment Voucher
Owns: suppliers' documents, purchase cost, what was bought
Module 3

Finance & Accounting

The books. Where every transaction in the business ends up as a number.

  • Chart of Accounts
  • Statement of Account
Owns: accounts, balances, the ledger
Module 4

Stock

What you hold, where it is, what it is worth, and every movement of it.

  • Inventory
  • Stock Movement
  • Stock Balance
  • Stock History
Owns: quantities, warehouses, stock value

Finance & Accounting and Stock are not the same thing

They are often confused because stock has a value and that value appears in the accounts. But Stock owns the quantities — how many boxes are in Zahle — and Finance owns the money. They exchange information; neither lives inside the other.

Keeping them apart is what lets you change how stock is counted without touching the books, and change the chart of accounts without touching the warehouse.

Section 5

Stock Movement — one screen, three kinds of movement

You asked whether Opening Stock, Stock In and Stock Out could be managed from one place. They can, and they should — but they stay three distinct transactions underneath.

Why one screen

Your storekeeper does one job — correcting what the shelf says. Making him find three different menu items to do it is the kind of design that gets worked around.

Why three transactions

They are not the same event. An opening balance is once and then locked. Damage is a loss. A correction is neither. If all three were one record, no report could ever tell them apart.

Every movement has a reason

"Stock went down by $400" is not useful. "Damaged, Beirut, by Nabil, on the 14th" is — and it is what the report shows.

One question still open on these screens

You wrote "with account to transfer charges." We have read that as the account the value of the goods is transferred to — so when four boxes are damaged, you choose where that loss lands in the accounts.

It could instead mean freight and handling charges added to the cost of the goods. Those are two different features. Tell us which you meant and we build that one.

Why this matters to you, not just to us

It is what makes the next change cheap. Adding Point of Sale later touches Sales and Stock — not Procurement, not the chart of accounts. Adding Payroll touches Finance only. A new module should not require opening files all over the system.

Section 7

They work together without becoming one thing

Integration means modules exchange agreed information at agreed points. It does not mean merging them.

What a hand-over looks like

Sales finishes an invoice and says: "this was sold, to this customer, for this much, and these goods left the warehouse." Stock reduces the quantity. Finance records the money. Neither of them had to know how a sales invoice works.

What it never looks like

Sales writing directly into the accounts, or Stock changing a customer's balance. Every module that did that would grow its own private version of the accounting rules — and removing that later is not a tidy-up, it is a rebuild.

Section 8

What still needs a decision from you

These are not delays we are hiding. Each one changes how something is built, and guessing would cost far more than asking.

The automatic bookkeeping is designed but not yet approved

Every screen in Phase 1 will be built. But the part that turns a sales invoice into accounting entries automatically — which account, in which direction, for which amount — is written down and waiting for a qualified accountant to review and sign it.

Until that signature, we do not code a single posting rule. A wrong accounting rule is invisible: it ships, the reports look right, and an auditor finds it a year later across eight companies. This is the one place where being slow is the cheap option.
